## Why this objective matters

Security architecture questions rarely ask which model is universally best, because no such model
exists. The exam gives you a business constraint — rapid scaling, strict physical separation,
limited patching, low latency, or recovery after failure — and expects you to identify the model
whose security implications fit it. The marks are in the trade-offs, not in memorising definitions.

<KeyPoint>
  Read every architecture scenario as two questions: **who controls each layer**, and **which
  constraint matters most**? A cloud provider may operate the hardware while the customer still owns
  identities and data. An isolated control system may reduce remote exposure while making patches
  and recovery harder.
</KeyPoint>

## Where the infrastructure lives

| Model       | Security advantage                                               | Security cost or constraint                                                    |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Cloud       | Elastic capacity, rapid deployment, provider-operated facilities | Shared duties, third-party dependency, configuration risk                      |
| On-premises | Direct control of hardware, location, and change timing          | The organisation funds, patches, powers, and recovers everything               |
| Hybrid      | Workloads can be placed according to sensitivity or capability   | Two environments, their identity systems, and their connection must be secured |

### Cloud responsibility is divided, not removed

A cloud **responsibility matrix** identifies which security tasks belong to the provider and which
belong to the customer. The exact boundary varies with the service, but outsourcing infrastructure
does not outsource accountability for every control. A provider can secure its physical facility
while a customer exposes data through an overly broad permission. On the exam, “it is in the cloud”
is never sufficient proof that a control is somebody else’s job.

**Hybrid considerations** include consistent identity, monitoring, data handling, and network policy
across on-premises and cloud resources. The connecting path is part of the architecture too.
**Third-party vendors** add dependency and supply-chain exposure. Contracts can assign duties and
transfer some financial impact, but the organisation still has to assess and monitor the risk.

<ExamTrap>
  Risk transference changes who absorbs an agreed loss; it does not make the vulnerability or
  operational dependency disappear. A contract is not a firewall, and insurance is not a patch.
</ExamTrap>

## How applications and control are organised

| Concept                      | What changes                                                  | Main security implication                                                                                |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Infrastructure as code (IaC) | Infrastructure is declared in versioned templates             | Repeatable review and deployment, but one flawed template can reproduce a weakness widely                |
| Serverless                   | The provider runs the underlying servers and runtime platform | Less host administration for the customer, but code, permissions, secrets, and data remain concerns      |
| Microservices                | An application is split into small communicating services     | Fault and deployment boundaries improve, while identities, APIs, and service-to-service traffic multiply |
| Centralized                  | Decisions or services converge on a central component         | Policy and visibility are consistent, but the centre can become a high-value target or failure point     |
| Decentralized                | Control or processing is distributed                          | Fewer single points of control, but consistency, inventory, and coordinated response become harder       |

IaC is powerful because the reviewed definition can be deployed repeatedly and compared with the
intended state. That same repetition creates **blast radius**: a publicly exposed port or excessive
permission in the template can be copied into every environment. Protect the repository, review
changes, scan templates, and prevent unapproved drift.

Serverless does not mean that servers cease to exist. It means the customer does not manage them.
The customer still secures application logic, event triggers, dependencies, permissions, secrets,
and data. Microservices likewise trade one large trust boundary for many smaller ones. Their APIs
need authentication and authorization, and their numerous dependencies require inventory and
monitoring.

<Mnemonic>
  **Managed is not ownerless.** The more a provider manages below the application, the more the
  customer’s attention moves upward to identities, configuration, code, and data.
</Mnemonic>

## Isolation and programmable networks

| Technique                         | Boundary                                      | Strength                                        | Limitation                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Physical isolation                | Separate hardware or cabling                  | Strong separation with few shared components    | Expensive and less flexible to operate                         |
| Air-gapped                        | No direct connection to other networks        | Greatly reduces network paths into the system   | Updates and data transfer need controlled alternate procedures |
| Logical segmentation              | Rules separate zones on shared infrastructure | Flexible and cheaper than dedicated hardware    | A rule or configuration error can weaken the boundary          |
| Software-defined networking (SDN) | Software centrally programs network behaviour | Rapid, consistent policy changes and automation | The controller and management plane become critical assets     |

An air gap is a special form of physical isolation focused on connectivity. It is not magical:
removable media, maintenance equipment, and people can still cross the boundary. Logical
segmentation uses mechanisms such as zones and policy enforcement on shared equipment. It can
contain compromise without purchasing a separate network for every trust level, provided the rules
are correct.

SDN separates network control from packet forwarding so policy can be programmed rather than
configured box by box. This improves responsiveness and consistency, but compromise or failure of
the control plane can affect a large part of the network. Protecting administrative access and
maintaining resilient controllers therefore matters.

## Containers and virtual machines

| Property        | Containerization                                            | Virtualization                                                             |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Isolation unit  | Processes and their packaged dependencies                   | Complete virtual machine with its own guest OS                             |
| Shared layer    | Host kernel is normally shared                              | Hypervisor and physical host are shared                                    |
| Typical benefit | Lightweight, fast start, consistent deployment              | Stronger separation and support for different guest operating systems      |
| Security focus  | Images, registries, orchestration, secrets, kernel boundary | Hypervisor, VM images, snapshots, guest patching, host resource contention |

Neither boundary should be treated as absolute. A vulnerable image can create many identical weak
instances, and a compromised orchestration or hypervisor layer can affect multiple workloads.
Compared with virtual machines, containers usually have less overhead but share more of the host.
Virtualization gives each guest an OS boundary, yet every guest still needs maintenance.

## Purpose-built and operational systems

| System                            | Defining need                                             | Security implication                                                       |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Internet of Things (IoT)          | Connected sensors and everyday devices                    | Large, diverse inventory; constrained interfaces and uneven update support |
| ICS/SCADA                         | Monitor or control physical and industrial processes      | Safety and availability may outweigh ordinary IT change speed              |
| Real-time operating system (RTOS) | Predictable response within timing constraints            | Security controls must not break deterministic timing                      |
| Embedded system                   | Computing built into a larger device for a narrow purpose | Long life, limited resources, and vendor-dependent maintenance             |

These categories overlap. An industrial sensor may be IoT, embedded, and controlled by an RTOS. The
exam distinguishes the reason for the design: IoT emphasises connectivity, ICS/SCADA physical
processes, RTOS timing guarantees, and embedded systems a dedicated function. Many such devices
cannot accept ordinary endpoint agents or frequent patches. Segmentation, restricted management
paths, monitoring, and compensating controls become important when direct patching is unavailable.

<InPractice>
  Rebooting an office laptop after a patch is routine. Rebooting a controller during a production
  cycle may stop machinery or affect safety. The same vulnerability can therefore lead to a
  different treatment because the architecture changes the consequence of the control.
</InPractice>

## High availability

High availability uses redundancy and failover to keep a service operating when a component fails.
It can include multiple nodes, paths, locations, or power sources. It improves availability, but it
does not replace backups: redundant systems can faithfully replicate corruption or a malicious
change. It can also increase attack surface and cost because there are more components to secure.

<ExamTrap>
  High availability answers “can the service continue now?” A backup answers “can earlier data be
  restored?” Both support resilience, but they solve different failure problems.
</ExamTrap>

## The twelve comparison lenses

| Consideration      | Question to ask                                                               |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Availability       | How much usable uptime does the design provide?                               |
| Resilience         | Can it absorb failure and continue or adapt?                                  |
| Cost               | What acquisition, operation, security, and recovery expense follows?          |
| Responsiveness     | How quickly does it react to workload, threat, or business change?            |
| Scalability        | Can capacity grow or shrink without redesign?                                 |
| Ease of deployment | How safely and consistently can it be introduced?                             |
| Risk transference  | Which consequences are contractually or financially shifted to another party? |
| Ease of recovery   | How difficult and slow is restoration after failure?                          |
| Patch availability | Does a maintained update exist when a flaw is found?                          |
| Inability to patch | Which compensating controls are needed when updating is impossible?           |
| Power              | What primary, backup, cooling, and site power dependencies exist?             |
| Compute            | Is there enough processing capacity for the workload and security controls?   |

Do not collapse neighbouring terms. **Availability** is the service being usable; **resilience** is
its ability to withstand and adapt to disruption; **ease of recovery** concerns restoration after
the disruption. **Responsiveness** concerns speed of reaction, while **scalability** concerns how
far capacity can change. **Patch availability** is a vendor or ecosystem property; **inability to
patch** is the operational condition that requires another treatment.

## Test yourself

1. A company moves an application to serverless computing. Which security responsibilities remain
   with the customer?
2. Which architecture provides a stronger separation boundary: logical segmentation or physical
   isolation, and what trade-off accompanies it?
3. A factory controller cannot be patched without interrupting a safety-critical process. Which
   consideration is present, and what kind of response is appropriate?

<KeyPoint>
  (1) Application code, permissions, secrets, dependencies, and data remain customer concerns even
  though the provider runs the servers. (2) Physical isolation provides the stronger boundary, at
  greater cost and with less flexibility. (3) This is inability to patch; use segmentation,
  restricted access, monitoring, and other compensating controls until a safe maintenance path
  exists.
</KeyPoint>
