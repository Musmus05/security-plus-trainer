## Why this objective matters

Audits and assessments turn a security claim into something a different person can examine. A team
may say that privileged access is reviewed, backups are protected, or a supplier meets a contractual
requirement. The useful question is not whether the claim sounds reassuring. It is who examined it,
what evidence was used, and who will rely on the result.

This objective is usually tested as a classification exercise. Read a scenario for the audience and
the degree of independence required, then distinguish internal work, external work, and an
attestation. Do not choose a label merely because an activity found a weakness: each type can find
one.

<KeyPoint>
  Start with the consumer of the result. Internal improvement suggests an internal audit or
  assessment. A customer or regulator that needs independent assurance suggests external work. A
  formal conclusion about stated criteria is an attestation.
</KeyPoint>

## Audit, assessment, and evidence

An **audit** is a structured examination against defined criteria. It gathers evidence, compares
that evidence with a policy, standard, contract, or framework, and reports a conclusion. An
**assessment** is a broader evaluation. It may measure maturity, identify gaps, test a control, or
recommend priorities without issuing the formal opinion associated with a particular audit.

The words overlap in ordinary conversation. On the exam, the clues matter more than a document
title. Look for a stated criterion, documented scope, evidence, findings, and an audience that will
act on the result. A review can be useful even when it does not issue a certificate or a formal
attestation.

| Question to ask                  | Audit                                          | Assessment                                               |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Main purpose                     | Determine conformance with defined criteria    | Understand condition, risk, or improvement opportunities |
| Typical output                   | Findings and a conclusion against the criteria | Observations, gaps, maturity view, or recommendations    |
| May be internal or external?     | Yes                                            | Yes                                                      |
| Automatically proves compliance? | No; it depends on the engagement               | No; it can support a later decision                      |

Evidence prevents a result from becoming mere opinion. It can include configuration records,
access-review evidence, tickets, logs, policies, interview answers, or observation of a process.
Good evidence is relevant to the criterion and sufficient for the conclusion being made. A signed
policy may demonstrate intended design, but it does not by itself prove that a control operates.
Likewise, a log without a defined requirement may not demonstrate that the right control exists.

<ExamTrap>
  Finding no problem during a review is not the same as proving that no problem exists. Every
  conclusion is bounded by its scope, criteria, period, and evidence. Never infer a guarantee the
  scenario did not give.
</ExamTrap>

## Internal audits and assessments

An **internal audit** is performed for the organisation itself. The reviewer may be an internal
audit function, a security team separate from the control owner, or another authorised group. Its
value is early and candid feedback: management can determine whether controls work as intended
before a customer, regulator, or incident exposes a gap.

Internal does not mean informal. A strong engagement has scope, criteria, evidence, findings,
remediation ownership, and follow-up. Independence still matters. The administrator who configured a
system should not be the only person judging whether it meets requirements. Separation reduces the
chance that self-interest or familiarity obscures a defect.

| Internal activity       | Useful purpose                                | Limitation to remember                          |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Control self-assessment | Lets owners identify gaps quickly             | It is not independent assurance by itself       |
| Internal audit          | Tests whether the organisation meets criteria | Its report primarily serves internal governance |
| Readiness assessment    | Prepares for a future external review         | Preparation is not the external review itself   |

Suppose a security director asks an internal team to compare quarterly access reviews with company
policy, sample completed reviews, and report exceptions to leadership. That is an internal audit. If
the same team interviews owners to identify immature areas and proposes a roadmap, it is an internal
assessment. Both are valuable; the expected conclusion differs.

<InPractice>
  Treat a finding as work with an owner and a due date. The audit or assessment identifies the
  issue; risk acceptance, remediation, and later verification are separate management actions.
</InPractice>

## External audits and assessments

An **external audit** is performed by a party outside the organisation. That party can be a
customer, regulator, certification body, or independent assessor, depending on purpose. The key exam
clue is independence from the organisation or control owner whose claim is examined.

External work can establish trust where an internal report cannot. A prospective customer may need
evidence before sharing data. A regulator may examine an applicable legal obligation. A
certification body may evaluate whether defined requirements are met. The external reviewer is not
automatically hostile; it simply has a different audience and assurance role.

| Situation                                                   | Most fitting activity        | Why                                                    |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Leadership wants a direct view before launching a program   | Internal assessment          | The result guides internal improvement                 |
| A customer requires independent evidence before contracting | External audit or assessment | The customer cannot rely only on the vendor's claim    |
| A regulator examines an obligation                          | External audit or assessment | The reviewer has authority outside the organisation    |
| A team checks its evidence before a review                  | Readiness assessment         | It prepares evidence; it is not independent validation |

External work may also produce recommendations. Do not equate “external” with “punitive,” or
“assessment” with “less rigorous.” The scenario's scope and intended reliance decide the answer.

## Attestation: a formal claim

An **attestation** is a formal statement that an assertion or set of controls meets specified
criteria. The word matters because another party will rely on the statement. The assertion might be
that controls were designed and operated according to requirements during a defined period. An
attestation ties together criteria, evidence, scope, and conclusion.

Attestation is not a manager saying “we are secure.” A self-declaration communicates a claim, but
does not automatically provide the independent assurance a customer or regulator expects. Likewise,
a penetration test can reveal technical weaknesses but is not, by itself, an attestation of an
entire control environment.

| Term           | Contribution                                       | Common confusion                             |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Assertion      | A claim that can be examined                       | A claim is not evidence                      |
| Attestation    | Formal conclusion about the claim against criteria | It is not a promise of perfect security      |
| Audit evidence | Support for the conclusion                         | A document can show design, not operation    |
| Scope          | Systems, period, and criteria covered              | A limited claim cannot safely be generalized |

<Mnemonic>
  **A-S-E:** Assertion, Scope, Evidence. Before trusting an attestation, ask what was claimed, what
  it covered, and what supported the conclusion.
</Mnemonic>

## Choosing the answer in a scenario

Use a short sequence. First identify the criteria: policy, contract, regulation, or framework.
Second identify who requested or will rely on the result. Third decide whether the scenario demands
independent assurance or internal improvement. Finally separate the activity from follow-up:
correcting a finding is remediation, not the audit itself.

For example, a cloud provider asks its internal compliance group to review access evidence before a
customer review. That work is a readiness assessment. If an independent party later examines the
same controls and issues a formal conclusion for customers to use, it is external work supporting an
attestation. The evidence may overlap, but the purpose and reliance do not.

<ExamTrap>
  “External” describes who performs the review, while “attestation” describes the purpose and form
  of the conclusion. They are not opposites: an independent external reviewer can perform work that
  supports an attestation.
</ExamTrap>
