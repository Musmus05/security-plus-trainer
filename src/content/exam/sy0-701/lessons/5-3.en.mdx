## Why this objective matters

Third parties can process customer records, operate a hosted application, support a critical device,
or connect directly to an organisation’s network. The organisation may delegate work, but it does
not delegate the consequences of a security failure. A capable supplier with weak controls can turn
an otherwise well-designed security programme into an exposed dependency.

The exam tests whether you can place a third-party control in the right part of its life cycle. A
scenario may describe a prospective cloud provider, a contract about to be signed, a supplier whose
access has changed, or a sanctioned security test. The useful question is: **are we assessing,
selecting, setting expectations, monitoring, or authorising work?** The neighbouring answers are
often all sensible activities, just at the wrong time.

<KeyPoint>
  Third-party risk management is continuous. Due diligence before selection establishes a baseline;
  agreements make obligations enforceable; monitoring checks whether the baseline still holds. A
  questionnaire is evidence collection, not evidence by itself.
</KeyPoint>

## Start with vendor assessment

A vendor assessment asks whether a specific relationship creates risk the organisation understands
and can accept. It is not merely a purchasing checklist. Start with what the vendor will do: the
data it will receive, the systems it will reach, the service it will provide, and the operational
impact if it fails. A supplier that prints public brochures deserves a different assessment from one
that hosts payroll data or administers production infrastructure.

| Assessment area        | Question to answer                                               | Useful evidence                              |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Data handling          | What data will be stored, processed, or transmitted?             | Data-flow description and retention practice |
| Access                 | Which accounts, interfaces, or facilities will the vendor reach? | Access design and least-privilege plan       |
| Security controls      | How does the vendor protect the service?                         | Policies, independent reports, test results  |
| Resilience             | What happens during an outage or incident?                       | Recovery arrangements and incident process   |
| Legal and business fit | Which obligations apply to this relationship?                    | Contract terms and applicable requirements   |

The depth of review should reflect the risk. This is not permission to skip assessment for a small
vendor; it means effort is proportionate. An inventory tool with no confidential data and no network
connection has a narrower risk profile than a payment processor. Assessment records let the owner
explain that judgement later and identify gaps needing remediation or acceptance.

<ExamTrap>
  A completed questionnaire does not prove that a control exists or works. It is the vendor’s
  statement about its environment. When the risk is material, ask for supporting evidence or use
  appropriate verification. Do not confuse a claim with assurance.
</ExamTrap>

## Select a vendor, not just a product

Vendor selection compares candidates against stated business and security requirements. Price,
features, delivery capacity, and security posture all matter. Selecting the cheapest supplier first
and discovering later that it cannot meet required controls reverses the process.

| Decision          | What it means                                             | Common wrong substitute                       |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Vendor assessment | Identify a candidate’s risks and control gaps             | Signing terms before understanding exposure   |
| Vendor selection  | Choose the candidate that best meets defined requirements | Treating lowest cost as the only criterion    |
| Risk acceptance   | Formally retain a known risk                              | Pretending an unaddressed gap disappeared     |
| Remediation       | Require a gap to be corrected                             | Monitoring a gap without an owner or deadline |

Selection should document why the chosen vendor is acceptable and what conditions remain. For
example, a candidate may be selected only after enabling multifactor authentication for support
access. The assessment identifies the gap; the selection decision and agreement establish the path
to address it. Security staff advise on risk, while the appropriate business owner accepts a
remaining business risk.

<InPractice>
  Procurement can make security review easier by involving it before a preferred supplier is
  announced. A late review tends to become a request to approve a decision already made, when the
  useful outcome may be comparing another candidate or changing the service design.
</InPractice>

## Agreements turn expectations into obligations

An agreement specifies what both parties owe one another. Exact names and clauses vary by
organisation and jurisdiction, so on the exam focus on the purpose of the document in the stem.

| Agreement type                    | Primary purpose                                                                | It does not replace                                              |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Service level agreement (SLA)     | Defines measurable service expectations, such as availability or response time | Security requirements for handling sensitive data                |
| Master service agreement (MSA)    | Sets broad commercial and legal terms for an ongoing relationship              | A detailed description of a particular piece of work             |
| Statement of work (SOW)           | Defines the specific work, deliverables, schedule, and responsibilities        | The overall contractual framework                                |
| Non-disclosure agreement (NDA)    | Limits disclosure of confidential information                                  | A complete service or security agreement                         |
| Memorandum of understanding (MOU) | Records a shared understanding between parties                                 | A detailed, enforceable operational contract where one is needed |

Security obligations can include notification of an incident, protection of data, limits on
subcontracting, audit or assessment rights, and return or destruction of data at the end of the
relationship. The key is that a requirement someone cannot be held to is only a preference. A
contract does not operate the controls for the vendor, but it establishes responsibility and a
remedy when expectations are missed.

<Mnemonic>
  **SLA = level of service; SOW = scope of work.** One says how the delivered service will be
  measured; the other says what work will be performed.
</Mnemonic>

## Monitor throughout the relationship

Risk changes after onboarding. The vendor may add a subcontractor, gain new access, move data to a
new region, suffer an incident, or let a certification lapse. Vendor monitoring is the recurring
process that looks for these changes and confirms that agreed controls and service obligations still
hold.

Monitoring can include periodic reassessments, review of assurance reports, performance against an
SLA, review of reported incidents, and confirmation that access remains appropriate. Frequency
should depend on criticality: a vendor with privileged production access needs more attention than a
low-impact office supplier. If monitoring finds a problem, follow the agreed escalation and
remediation process; simply collecting a report is not management.

<ExamTrap>
  Monitoring is not a one-time selection task. A question that says “the vendor was approved two
  years ago” but describes changed services, expired evidence, or recurring failures is asking for
  ongoing monitoring or reassessment.
</ExamTrap>

## Questionnaires and rules of engagement

A questionnaire makes assessment repeatable. It asks comparable questions about data handling,
access control, incident response, resilience, and other relevant practices. It helps screen many
vendors and identify where deeper investigation is needed. A generic questionnaire copied to every
vendor can miss the relationship’s actual risk, so tailor it to the service and use the answers to
drive follow-up.

Rules of engagement are different. They authorise and bound an activity such as a penetration test
or other security assessment. Before testing, the parties agree on target systems, permitted
techniques, timing, points of contact, data handling, emergency stop conditions, and how findings
will be reported. Those limits protect both sides: a tester needs clear permission, and the vendor
needs to know when unusual activity is legitimate.

| If the scenario says…                                                 | Best concept        |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| “How do we gather comparable security information from bidders?”      | Questionnaire       |
| “Who may scan these systems, when, and how far may they go?”          | Rules of engagement |
| “How quickly must the provider restore its service?”                  | SLA                 |
| “Does the provider still meet our requirements after a major change?” | Vendor monitoring   |

## Test yourself

1. A provider says it has encryption and incident response. What makes that claim useful in a
   high-risk assessment?
2. Which agreement most directly defines a measurable service availability target: an SLA or an SOW?
3. Before an authorised test of a vendor-hosted application, what document defines targets,
   permitted actions, and stop conditions?

<KeyPoint>
  (1) Supporting evidence or appropriate verification; a questionnaire answer alone is a claim. (2)
  An SLA. (3) Rules of engagement.
</KeyPoint>
