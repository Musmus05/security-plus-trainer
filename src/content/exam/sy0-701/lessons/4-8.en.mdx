## Why this objective matters

An incident is not finished when an alert is closed. On the exam, this objective tests whether you
can choose the activity that makes a response deliberate, repeatable, and useful after an event. The
useful distinction is usually timing: preparation happens before an incident, containment and
investigation happen during it, and learning happens afterwards. A tempting answer may be a real
security activity but belong to the wrong point in that cycle.

Incident response is also more than a technical team removing malware. It coordinates people,
evidence, decisions, communications, and recovery while protecting the business. An organisation
that can detect an attack but cannot preserve evidence, contact the right people, or restore a
service safely has only solved part of the problem.

<KeyPoint>
  Read the scenario for its purpose. Is the team preparing people, rehearsing a plan, finding the
  cause, proactively searching for hidden activity, or preserving evidence? That purpose points to
  the tested activity.
</KeyPoint>

## The incident response process

Different frameworks use slightly different labels, but the process has a consistent shape. The exam
is interested in the outcome of each phase, not in memorising one vendor's diagram.

| Phase                  | Primary goal                                              | Typical output                                             |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Preparation            | Be ready to respond before an event                       | Plan, roles, contacts, tooling, and playbooks              |
| Detection and analysis | Decide whether the event is an incident and understand it | Scope, affected assets, severity, and evidence             |
| Containment            | Limit damage and stop spread                              | Isolated hosts, blocked indicators, preserved access paths |
| Eradication            | Remove the cause and attacker foothold                    | Removed malware, disabled accounts, corrected weakness     |
| Recovery               | Return services to a trustworthy operating state          | Restored systems, monitoring, and business validation      |
| Lessons learned        | Improve the next response                                 | Findings, assigned actions, and updated procedures         |

Containment is not eradication. Disconnecting an infected endpoint can stop it reaching other
systems, but it does not prove that the malicious persistence mechanism is gone. Eradication may
require rebuilding the endpoint, removing a rogue account, resetting secrets, or correcting the
vulnerability that admitted the attacker. Recovery comes only when the organisation can operate
again with justified confidence, often while heightened monitoring watches for recurrence.

<ExamTrap>
  "Turn off the compromised server" is usually containment, not recovery. It reduces immediate
  exposure but does not restore the business service. Conversely, restoring a backup without
  removing the original access path can recreate the incident.
</ExamTrap>

### Roles and decisions

The incident response plan should identify an incident commander, technical responders, system
owners, legal or privacy contacts, communications staff, and escalation paths. Not every incident
needs every role, but deciding who may isolate a critical system or notify an external party during
the event wastes precious time. A plan also establishes severity criteria, documentation practices,
communication channels, and decision authority.

A playbook is a focused, repeatable procedure for a common event such as phishing, ransomware, a
lost device, or suspicious cloud access. It does not replace judgement: a playbook gives responders
a safe starting sequence and a recordable decision path. If a prompt says responders need known
contacts, authority, and steps before an attack, the answer is preparation through an incident
response plan or playbook, not a post-incident review.

## Training and testing are different

Training prepares people; testing evaluates the plan and exposes gaps. Both belong before a real
incident, but they answer different questions.

| Activity               | What it develops or proves                                       | Example                                                                |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Training               | Individual knowledge and role competence                         | An analyst learns how to classify a suspected phishing report          |
| Tabletop exercise      | Decision-making and communication in a discussion                | Leaders walk through a ransomware scenario without changing systems    |
| Simulation             | Response against a realistic, controlled event                   | A team investigates staged malicious activity in a test environment    |
| Parallel test          | Whether an alternate capability can operate alongside production | Recovery staff run a standby process without interrupting the live one |
| Full interruption test | Whether the plan works under actual failover conditions          | A scheduled exercise transfers a service to its recovery site          |

The least disruptive test is usually a tabletop exercise. It is valuable for clarifying who calls
whom and who has authority, but it cannot demonstrate that an endpoint isolation tool works or that
a backup can be restored. A full interruption test gives stronger operational evidence, at higher
business risk. Choose the test whose realism matches the question's need.

<InPractice>
  After an exercise, record the observable result: the paging list was outdated, a responder could
  not reach a console, or legal approval took too long. Assign an owner and deadline. An exercise
  without tracked improvements is rehearsal without learning.
</InPractice>

## Root cause analysis

Root cause analysis asks why the incident became possible, not merely what alert appeared first. For
example, deleting a phishing email treats a symptom. The root cause might include a missing email
control, an unpatched browser, excessive user permissions, weak review of a payment process, or
several connected failures. The evidence may show an initial access vector, but responders must
avoid declaring that vector the root cause before the investigation supports it.

Useful methods include asking successive "why" questions, tracing a timeline, and comparing expected
controls with what actually happened. The result should identify corrective actions that prevent or
reduce recurrence. Root cause analysis is retrospective and improvement-oriented; it is not the same
as threat hunting, which looks proactively for undiscovered malicious activity.

<Mnemonic>
  **Contain, clean, recover, learn**: limit the damage; remove the foothold; restore trusted
  operation; then learn why the event occurred and improve the response.
</Mnemonic>

## Threat hunting

Threat hunting is a proactive, hypothesis-driven search for adversary activity that ordinary alerts
have not surfaced. A hunter might ask whether a newly reported technique has appeared anywhere in
the environment, then query endpoint, identity, DNS, and network telemetry for its traces. This is
not simply watching a dashboard: monitoring waits for configured conditions, whereas hunting begins
with a question and investigates across data sources.

Hunting can be triggered by intelligence, an incident elsewhere, a suspicious pattern, or a regular
programme. It may find no compromise; a well-scoped negative result still improves confidence and
can refine detections. If it finds activity, the finding enters the incident response process, where
the team validates scope, contains it, and preserves relevant evidence.

| Term                   | Core distinction                                                 |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Monitoring             | Continuously observes known signals and raises configured alerts |
| Threat hunting         | Proactively searches for hidden or unknown malicious behavior    |
| Incident investigation | Determines facts and scope after a suspected or confirmed event  |
| Root cause analysis    | Explains the enabling failures after the response is stable      |

## Digital forensics

Digital forensics collects, preserves, examines, and reports digital evidence so conclusions can be
defended. Evidence can include disk images, volatile memory, authentication records, network
captures, cloud audit data, and mobile-device artefacts. The required collection depends on the
question, but the core principle is integrity: do not change the evidence unnecessarily and be able
to explain who handled it.

Chain of custody records the evidence identifier, who collected it, when and where it was obtained,
how it was stored, every transfer, and who received it. Hash values can demonstrate that an acquired
image has not changed. An original system may be critical to operations, so investigators commonly
work from a verified copy while preserving the original according to procedure.

<ExamTrap>
  Rebooting a system can destroy volatile memory and change logs. If the scenario prioritises
  investigation or legal defensibility, preserve evidence and document actions before taking steps
  that overwrite it, while still balancing urgent containment needs.
</ExamTrap>

Forensics is not automatically a criminal investigation. Its findings can support technical
recovery, internal discipline, insurance, regulatory response, or litigation. The same disciplined
evidence handling makes all of those outcomes more reliable.
