## Why this objective matters

This objective opens domain 2, the heaviest on the exam at 22%. It contains no techniques at all:
only **who attacks, with what resources, and why**.

It is deceptively simple. The actors look obvious until a scenario describes an attack and asks who
most likely carried it out. The answer never follows from the technique used — it follows from the
**combination of resources and motivation**.

<KeyPoint>
  Do not ask "which attack". Ask **"who wanted this, and who could afford it"**. Two actors may use
  exactly the same tool; what separates them is what they want and what they can pay for.
</KeyPoint>

## The actors

| Actor              | Resources      | Sophistication | Dominant motivation                |
| ------------------ | -------------- | -------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Nation-state       | Near-unlimited | Very high      | Espionage, war, disruption         |
| Organized crime    | High           | High           | Financial gain                     |
| Insider threat     | Varies         | Varies         | Grievance, financial gain, mistake |
| Hacktivist         | Low            | Medium         | Political or philosophical belief  |
| Shadow IT          | Internal       | Low            | **None** — they are not attackers  |
| Unskilled attacker | Very low       | Very low       | Notoriety, curiosity, chaos        |

<ExamTrap>
  Shadow IT is listed among the threat actors but has **no malicious intent**. These are colleagues
  routing around official IT to get their work done faster. The risk is real, the intent is not —
  and a question that stresses the absence of malice is very probably describing this.
</ExamTrap>

<ExamTrap>
  "Unskilled attacker" replaced the older term "script kiddie". Both mean someone running tools they
  do not understand. What matters: they are unpredictable and noisy, so easy to detect, but they can
  still do damage with a borrowed tool.
</ExamTrap>

## The attributes that separate them

The exam keeps three attributes, and it combines them.

**Internal or external** — an insider already holds legitimate access. They do not need to breach a
perimeter; they are inside it. That is what makes insider threat hard to detect, not their
sophistication.

**Resources and funding** — the only attribute that explains an attack sustained over months. A
hacktivist cannot buy a seven-figure unknown vulnerability; a state can.

**Level of sophistication and capability** — the ability to build their own tools rather than run
somebody else's.

<Mnemonic>
  **Quiet, slow, bespoke, running for months → a nation-state.** Nobody else funds that much
  patience. Loud and immediate → somebody with nothing to lose.
</Mnemonic>

## The motivations

| Motivation                      | The scenario it produces                        |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Financial gain                  | Ransomware, fraud, resale of data               |
| Data exfiltration               | Silent theft, no degradation                    |
| Espionage                       | Industrial or state secrets, over the long term |
| Service disruption              | Denial of service, sabotage                     |
| Blackmail                       | Threat of disclosure                            |
| Philosophical/political beliefs | Website defacement, activist leak               |
| Revenge                         | Dismissed employee, targeted sabotage           |
| Disruption/chaos                | Destruction with no benefit to the author       |
| War                             | Attack on critical infrastructure               |
| Ethical                         | Security researcher, responsible disclosure     |

<KeyPoint>
  **Ethical** motivation is on the official list. A researcher who reports a flaw is an actor in the
  sense of this objective, even though they are helping. A question describing an intrusion followed
  by a detailed report sent to the company is not describing a criminal.
</KeyPoint>

## Reading a scenario correctly

The exam builds these questions by giving two clues: one about means, one about ends. The
distractors are the actors who have one but not the other.

<InPractice>
  "An intrusion discovered after fourteen months, no data destroyed, only research documents
  copied."

Fourteen months undetected implies considerable funding and discipline: that rules out the unskilled
attacker and the hacktivist. Nothing is monetised quickly: that rules out organized crime, whose
model is yield. Research documents taken quietly over time is espionage — so a nation-state.

</InPractice>

<ExamTrap>
  Organized crime and nation-states both have deep resources. What separates them is **tempo**:
  organized crime monetises fast, because time costs money. A state can wait years.
</ExamTrap>

## Insider threat, the special case

An insider does not have to cross a perimeter, which makes most network controls useless against
them. The countermeasures therefore live elsewhere: least privilege, separation of duties, job
rotation, access reviews, and the accounting seen in 1.2.

Not every insider threat is malicious. An employee who clicks a phishing link is a vector, not an
actor — a distinction objective 2.2 picks up.

## Test yourself

1. A government website is defaced with a political message, in broad daylight, with no data stolen.
   Which actor?
2. Which listed actor has no malicious intent at all?
3. Two actors both have deep resources. What distinguishes them in a scenario?

<KeyPoint>
  (1) A hacktivist — visibility **is** the goal, and there is no financial gain. (2) Shadow IT. (3)
  Tempo: organized crime monetises fast, a nation-state can wait years.
</KeyPoint>
