## Why this objective matters

The exam does not reward a shopping list of security products here. It gives you an enterprise
constraint—an exposed web application, an untrusted workstation, a remote administrator, or a
control whose failure must not interrupt production—and asks you to place or select the control that
reduces the relevant risk. The marks come from understanding where a control sits, what traffic it
can observe, and what happens when it fails.

<KeyPoint>
  Start every infrastructure scenario with three questions: **what asset is being protected, where
  is the trust boundary, and must traffic pass through the control?** Those answers usually remove
  most distractors before product features matter.
</KeyPoint>

## Infrastructure considerations

### Placement, zones, and paths

Device placement determines whether a control can see or stop the traffic that matters. An internet
firewall belongs at the external boundary; an internal segmentation firewall belongs between zones
with different trust levels; a web application firewall belongs in the path to web applications.
Putting a capable device on the wrong path leaves a blind spot.

| Consideration    | Question to ask                                                            | Typical mistake                                           |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Device placement | Does the relevant traffic actually cross the control?                      | Installing a sensor where it sees only unrelated traffic  |
| Security zones   | Which systems share a trust level and which flows cross a boundary?        | Treating the internal network as one trusted zone         |
| Attack surface   | Which reachable services, interfaces, identities, and paths can be abused? | Adding a control while leaving an alternate path open     |
| Connectivity     | Which business flows must remain possible?                                 | Blocking required dependencies along with hostile traffic |

A security zone is a policy boundary, not merely a subnet name. Public services may occupy a DMZ,
user devices a workstation zone, databases a restricted zone, and management interfaces a highly
controlled management zone. Segmentation limits lateral movement only when traffic between zones is
filtered. A diagram with several VLANs but unrestricted routing has labels, not meaningful security
boundaries.

Reducing attack surface means removing unnecessary exposure: disable unused services, restrict
management interfaces, close unneeded ports, and avoid duplicate paths around inspection. It does
not mean eliminating all connectivity. Effective architecture preserves the smallest set of flows
needed by the business.

<ExamTrap>
  A control protects only traffic it can observe. A network IDS connected to the wrong switch, or a
  WAF bypassed by a second public address, does not become effective because its policy is correct.
</ExamTrap>

### Failure mode and device behavior

| Pair                    | First choice                                 | Second choice                     | Security trade-off                                          |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fail-open / fail-closed | Traffic continues if the device fails        | Traffic stops if the device fails | Availability versus enforcement                             |
| Active / passive        | The device can act on traffic                | The device observes and reports   | Prevention versus low operational impact                    |
| Inline / tap or monitor | Production traffic passes through the device | A copy of traffic is sent to it   | Ability to block versus resistance to becoming a bottleneck |

Fail-closed preserves the policy when a device fails, but may turn one failed appliance into an
outage. Fail-open preserves connectivity, but permits traffic without that enforcement point. The
right answer follows the scenario: a high-security gateway may favor fail-closed, while a
safety-critical process may require availability and compensating monitoring.

Inline describes placement; active describes behavior. They often occur together, as with an inline
IPS, but they are not synonyms. A passive IDS commonly receives copied traffic from a network tap or
switch port mirror. It cannot reliably stop the packet already in flight, but its failure does not
interrupt the production path.

<Mnemonic>
  **Inline is where; active is what.** Inline traffic goes through the control. An active control
  takes action. Keep location and behavior as separate decisions.
</Mnemonic>

## Infrastructure enforcement points

### Appliances with neighbouring jobs

| Control       | Primary job                                                     | Best placement or use                                   |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Jump server   | Controlled administrative entry point                           | Between administrators and a restricted management zone |
| Proxy server  | Intermediates client requests and applies access policy         | Between clients and external or protected services      |
| IDS           | Detects and alerts on suspicious activity                       | Monitor or tap where relevant traffic can be copied     |
| IPS           | Detects and blocks suspicious activity                          | Inline where protected traffic must cross it            |
| Load balancer | Distributes requests among service instances                    | In front of a server pool for capacity and availability |
| Sensor        | Collects network, host, physical, or environmental observations | Close enough to the event or traffic being measured     |

A jump server concentrates privileged access so it can be hardened, strongly authenticated, and
logged. It is not a general browsing proxy. A proxy represents one side of a connection to the
other: a forward proxy commonly controls client access outward, while a reverse proxy fronts
servers. A load balancer improves distribution and resilience; security inspection may be an added
feature, but it is not the defining purpose.

IDS and IPS are distinguished by response and path. An IDS provides evidence and alerts without
being the enforcement point. An IPS can reject malicious traffic because it is inline and active.
Choosing IPS adds blocking power and also raises the consequences of false positives and device
failure.

### Port access and firewall scope

IEEE 802.1X provides port-based network access control. A supplicant requests access, an
authenticator such as a switch or access point controls the port, and an authentication server
validates the request. Extensible Authentication Protocol (EAP) is the framework that carries an
authentication method. EAP is not itself a firewall rule or a VPN.

Port security can also restrict which endpoints may use a switch port. The goal is to stop an
unapproved device from gaining ordinary network access merely because a live socket is available.

| Firewall type                   | Best distinction                                                           |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Layer 4 firewall                | Filters mainly by addresses, protocol, ports, and connection state         |
| Layer 7 firewall                | Understands application-level requests and content                         |
| Web application firewall (WAF)  | Specializes in HTTP/S traffic and attacks against web applications         |
| Unified threat management (UTM) | Bundles several security functions into one appliance or service           |
| Next-generation firewall (NGFW) | Adds application awareness and advanced inspection to firewall enforcement |

A WAF is the precise choice for malicious HTTP requests aimed at application logic; it does not
replace network segmentation for databases. A Layer 4 rule can efficiently allow TCP 443, but it
does not understand whether the permitted HTTPS request contains an application attack. UTM
emphasizes consolidated functions, while NGFW emphasizes application-aware enforcement and deeper
inspection. Product labels overlap, so exam questions usually provide a defining requirement.

<ExamTrap>
  Port 443 is not proof that traffic is safe. A Layer 4 control can permit the connection because
  the port is allowed; a Layer 7 control or WAF examines what the application request is doing.
</ExamTrap>

## Secure communication and access

Secure remote access protects both the channel and the decision about who may use it. A virtual
private network (VPN) creates a protected logical connection over an untrusted network. Remote
access is the broader business capability; a VPN is one way to provide its secure channel.

| Method            | What it protects or changes                                                        | Key distinction                                                   |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| TLS tunnel        | Protects an application session, commonly over TCP                                 | Often used for browser-based or application-specific access       |
| IPsec tunnel      | Protects IP packets at the network layer                                           | Common for site-to-site and full network remote access            |
| Remote-access VPN | Connects an individual user or device to enterprise resources                      | Identity and endpoint policy matter alongside encryption          |
| Site-to-site VPN  | Connects networks through protected gateways                                       | Users may not initiate the tunnel individually                    |
| SD-WAN            | Selects and manages wide-area paths through software-defined policy                | Optimizes branch connectivity across multiple links               |
| SASE              | Delivers networking and security controls as a cloud-oriented service architecture | Brings access policy closer to distributed users and applications |

Tunneling encapsulates one kind of traffic inside another. Encryption and tunneling are related but
not identical: a tunnel defines a path and encapsulation, while the chosen protocol determines the
security properties. TLS and IPsec can provide confidentiality, integrity, and peer authentication
when correctly configured.

Software-defined wide area networking (SD-WAN) improves centralized path selection and branch
connectivity, but it is not automatically a complete security architecture. Secure access service
edge (SASE) combines wide-area access with cloud-delivered security capabilities for distributed
users, sites, and applications. In a scenario, choose SD-WAN when path management is the dominant
requirement and SASE when consistent security policy for widely distributed access is central.

<InPractice>
  Encrypting a remote session protects it in transit; it does not make the remote administrator
  trustworthy. Strong authentication, least privilege, device posture, logging, and a controlled
  management path still apply after the tunnel is established.
</InPractice>

## Selecting effective controls

“Most advanced” is not the same as effective. The effective control addresses the stated threat,
sits on the relevant path, fits the required availability, and produces an acceptable operational
cost. Use the clue in the scenario to select the narrowest control that solves the problem.

| Scenario clue                                        | Strong candidate                   | Why neighbouring choices fall short                               |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Block malicious requests to a public web application | WAF in the application path        | A basic Layer 4 rule sees the permitted port, not the request     |
| Detect suspicious traffic without risking an outage  | Passive IDS on a tap or mirror     | Inline IPS can block, but can also interrupt traffic              |
| Force administrators through one audited route       | Jump server in the management zone | A VPN protects transit but does not centralize sessions by itself |
| Reject unauthenticated devices at a wired port       | 802.1X                             | A firewall sees later flows, after attachment has occurred        |
| Keep service available across several back ends      | Load balancer                      | An IDS observes attacks but does not distribute demand            |
| Protect traffic between two branch networks          | IPsec site-to-site VPN             | TLS commonly protects a narrower application session              |

Defense in depth may combine several of these, but each control needs a reason. More devices add
management interfaces, dependencies, cost, and failure modes—an attack surface of their own.
Validate placement, monitor the control, test its failure behavior, and review whether business
traffic has found an uninspected bypass.

## Test yourself

1. An organization wants visibility into a critical link but cannot let a sensor failure interrupt
   production. What placement and behavior fit best?
2. A public application already allows HTTPS through a Layer 4 firewall. Which control should
   inspect malicious HTTP requests?
3. What distinguishes fail-open from fail-closed, and why is neither universally correct?

<KeyPoint>
  (1) A passive IDS or sensor fed by a tap or port mirror; it observes a copy rather than sitting in
  the production path. (2) A WAF placed in the application traffic path. (3) Fail-open preserves
  connectivity without enforcement, while fail-closed preserves enforcement by stopping traffic; the
  correct choice depends on the scenario’s availability and security priorities.
</KeyPoint>
