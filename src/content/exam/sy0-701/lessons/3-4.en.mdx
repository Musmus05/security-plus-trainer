## Why this objective matters

Security architecture is judged when something fails. A sound firewall or encryption design still
fails the organisation if one server outage, flooded site, exhausted resource, or untested restore
stops a critical service. Resilience is the ability to continue or recover an acceptable service
after disruption; recovery is the deliberate route back to the required state.

The exam tests whether the proposed design addresses the disruption in the scenario. Read the clue
carefully: a second component addresses a component failure, another site addresses a site loss, a
restore addresses lost or corrupted data, and a tested procedure addresses uncertainty about whether
the plan will work. These controls are related, but they are not interchangeable.

<KeyPoint>
  Begin with the failed dependency: **service** means availability, **site** means location
  strategy, **data** means backup and restoration, **power** means electrical continuity, and **an
  unproven plan** means testing. The best answer normally names the control nearest to that
  dependency.
</KeyPoint>

## High availability: keep the service reachable

**High availability (HA)** reduces interruption when an individual component, host, or path fails.
Redundancy only helps when the replacement can take over without sharing the same failure. Two
application servers behind a load balancer can serve requests when one host stops; two virtual
machines on the same failed host cannot.

| Design idea          | What it addresses                                 | Important limit                                |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Redundant components | Failure of one component or connection            | A shared dependency can still fail both        |
| Load balancing       | Distribution of requests across healthy instances | It does not restore damaged data               |
| Failover             | Moving work to a standby service after failure    | The standby must be ready and reachable        |
| Clustering           | Coordinated service from multiple members         | It does not automatically protect another site |

Availability is not a claim that outages are impossible. It is a decision about acceptable
interruption and how the design detects failure, directs traffic, and recovers capacity. A scenario
where users must continue after one server fails points to HA, not simply to a backup. A backup
helps recover information later; it does not normally take live requests at the moment of failure.

<ExamTrap>
  Redundancy is not automatically resilience. Two devices in one rack can improve hardware
  availability, yet both can be lost to the same power event, fire, or network failure. Ask which
  dependencies the supposedly separate copies still share.
</ExamTrap>

## Site considerations: recover beyond one location

A disruption can be bigger than a server: a building can lose power, become inaccessible, or suffer
an environmental event. **Site considerations** compare where recovery work can occur and how much
capability is prepared there. Business impact and continuity requirements determine the choice; no
site type is universally best.

| Site type             | Prepared capability                                              | Recovery trade-off                         |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Hot site              | Equipped and ready to assume operations quickly                  | Highest ongoing cost                       |
| Warm site             | Some infrastructure is prepared; systems or data need completion | Moderate cost and recovery time            |
| Cold site             | Basic facility with little ready computing capability            | Lowest cost and longest setup              |
| Geographic separation | Recovery resources are away from the primary site                | Adds coordination and replication concerns |

A hot site is not merely a room with spare equipment: it is prepared for rapid operation. A cold
site is not a failed primary site; it is a planned alternate location needing setup. Geographic
separation matters because an alternate site near the primary can share the same regional event.
However, placing systems far apart can introduce latency, replication, and operational complexity.

<InPractice>
  Map a service dependency before declaring a second site ready. The alternate needs more than
  servers: it may need identity access, network connectivity, current data, supplier contacts, and
  people who know how to operate it.
</InPractice>

## Platform diversity and multi-cloud systems

**Platform diversity** reduces dependence on a single implementation, vendor, operating environment,
or technology pattern. It can limit the impact of one platform-specific defect or compromise. It
also adds skills, monitoring, integration, and configuration work. Diversity is valuable when it
removes a meaningful common failure mode, not when it merely makes every system different.

**Multi-cloud systems** use services from more than one cloud provider. They can support resilience
against a provider-specific outage and can help meet regional or service requirements. They do not
automatically create high availability: the application must use the alternatives, data must be
available where needed, and identity and network controls must work across providers.

| Approach                 | Resilience benefit                                 | New responsibility                                  |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Platform diversity       | Reduces exposure to one shared technology weakness | Maintain secure, compatible different platforms     |
| Multi-cloud              | Reduces reliance on one cloud provider             | Coordinate identity, data, networking, and recovery |
| Single standard platform | Simplifies operations and hardening                | A platform-wide failure can affect more services    |

<ExamTrap>
  Multi-cloud describes provider choice, whereas geographic redundancy describes location. A
  workload in two regions of one provider can survive a regional loss; workloads in two providers in
  the same vulnerable area may not. The scenario decides which distinction matters.
</ExamTrap>

## Continuity of operations and capacity planning

**Continuity of operations** is the ability to sustain essential business functions during a
disruption. It includes people, procedures, communications, alternate work arrangements, and the
technical services those functions need. Disaster recovery is an important technical contribution,
but continuity is broader: a recovered application is of little use if its operators cannot access
it or the business has no workable process for the disruption.

**Capacity planning** ensures resources remain sufficient as demand changes and during failure.
Normal usage is an incomplete measurement. If one node fails, the remaining nodes must carry the
load; if traffic surges during an incident, bandwidth, compute, storage, and licences must still be
adequate. Monitoring trends and testing realistic failure conditions turn capacity assumptions into
evidence.

<Mnemonic>
  **Continuity keeps the business working; capacity keeps the working design from running out.** One
  answers “can we operate?” and the other answers “can the surviving resources carry it?”
</Mnemonic>

## Testing and backups: prove recovery before needing it

Plans, replicas, and backups create confidence only after testing. Testing can check that contacts
are current, a failover actually routes traffic, a recovery site can operate, and restored data is
usable. A tabletop exercise tests decisions and communication; a technical recovery test provides
stronger evidence that systems and data can be restored. The right test scope balances risk and
disruption, but an untested plan remains an assumption.

Backups preserve recoverable copies of data. Their value depends on scope, retention, protection,
and the ability to restore the right copy. Replication can improve availability by keeping another
copy current, but it can also copy deletion or corruption quickly. A backup with suitable history
can provide a recovery point when a live replica cannot.

| Need in the scenario                                | Best first concept            |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| A service must continue after host failure          | High availability or failover |
| A building is unavailable                           | Alternate-site planning       |
| Data was deleted or corrupted                       | Backup and restoration        |
| Nobody knows whether the recovery plan works        | Testing                       |
| Remaining systems become overloaded after a failure | Capacity planning             |

<ExamTrap>
  A successful backup job proves that data was copied, not that it can be restored within the
  required time or used by the application. Restore testing proves the recovery outcome.
</ExamTrap>

## Power: preserve the foundation

Power resilience considers outages, short interruptions, and unstable electrical supply. An
**uninterruptible power supply (UPS)** can bridge a short loss and allow orderly shutdown or time
for another source to start. A generator can sustain service longer, subject to fuel, maintenance,
and safe operation. Redundant power feeds and conditioned power can reduce single points of failure,
but they must be designed around actual facility dependencies.

Do not confuse power protection with backup. A UPS protects running equipment during an electrical
event; a backup protects a recoverable copy of data. An orderly shutdown may prevent corruption, but
it is not the same as retaining a separate historical copy.

   is most directly relevant?
2. Why can a current replica be insufficient after accidental deletion?

<KeyPoint>
  (1) High availability: redundancy and failover can keep requests served. (2) Replication can copy
  the deletion, while a retained backup may provide an earlier recovery point. (3) A UPS bridges a
  short power event or supports shutdown; a generator is intended to provide longer-running power.
</KeyPoint>
