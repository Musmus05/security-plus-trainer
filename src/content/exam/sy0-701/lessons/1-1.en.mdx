## Why this objective matters

It is the first objective on the exam, and it is also the one that turns up most often in disguise.
A question about firewalls, another about staff training, a third about an access vestibule — in all
three cases what you are really being asked is _what kind of control is this_.

CompTIA classifies every security control on **two independent axes**:

- the **category** — _where_ the control lives;
- the **type** — _what it does_.

Every control has both. A firewall is technical in category and preventive in type. An acceptable
use policy is managerial and directive. Confusing the two axes is the single most common mistake on
this objective, and the exam tests it directly.

<KeyPoint>
  Category = **where**. Type = **what it does**. Two separate questions, and every control answers
  both.
</KeyPoint>

## The four categories

| Category    | Where the control lives       | Examples                                                             |
| ----------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Technical   | Hardware, software, firmware  | Firewall rule, ACL, disk encryption, MFA, antivirus                  |
| Managerial  | Decisions, governance, policy | Acceptable use policy, risk assessment, background-check requirement |
| Operational | Activities people carry out   | Guard patrol, daily log review, backup rotation                      |
| Physical    | The tangible world            | Fence, bollard, lock, access vestibule, lighting                     |

The managerial category is also called **administrative** in much of the literature; the two words
mean the same thing.

### The managerial / operational boundary

This is the one category boundary the exam genuinely exploits, because it separates two things that
share a name in ordinary speech.

> The **policy** requiring daily log review is **managerial**. The **technician** who performs it
> each morning is **operational**.

Same subject, two distinct controls. Whenever a question mentions a requirement _and_ its execution,
work out which of the two it names.

<ExamTrap>
  "Security awareness training" is ambiguous on purpose. The training *programme* and its
  requirement are managerial; *delivering* the session each quarter is operational. Read which part
  the question names.
</ExamTrap>

## The six types

| Type         | What it does                                         | When           | Example                                                            |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Preventive   | Stops the action                                     | Before         | Lock, deny rule, encryption, training                              |
| Deterrent    | Discourages the attempt                              | Before         | "Area under surveillance" sign, visible guard, prosecution warning |
| Detective    | Reports what happened                                | During / after | Camera, SIEM, log review, IDS                                      |
| Corrective   | Restores the expected state                          | After          | Backup restore, quarantine, patch applied                          |
| Compensating | Substitutes for a control that cannot be implemented | Instead of     | Jump host in front of an application that cannot do MFA            |
| Directive    | States what should be done                           | Before         | Procedure, standard, "Visitors must sign in" sign                  |

### Preventive versus deterrent

A **preventive** control makes the action impossible. A **deterrent** makes it unattractive. The
question to ask: _can a determined attacker simply walk past it?_

- A locked door: no → **preventive**.
- An "Area under video surveillance" sign: yes, they walk straight past → **deterrent**.

<Mnemonic>
  **Deterrent lives in the head.** It stops nothing physically; it changes a mind. If a control
  exists only in order to be *seen*, it is a deterrent.
</Mnemonic>

### Directive versus deterrent

Both address a human, and after compensating/corrective this is the most confused pair.

- "Trespassers will be prosecuted" → threatens a consequence → **deterrent**.
- "Visitors must sign in at reception" → states the expectation → **directive**.

A directive control says _what to do_. A deterrent says _what will happen if you do not_.

### Compensating versus corrective

This is **the** pair the exam leans on hardest here. The distinction is not what the control is, but
**what triggered it**.

|         | Compensating                                                              | Corrective                                         |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Trigger | "We cannot do the required thing"                                         | "Something has gone wrong"                         |
| Timing  | Instead of the missing control                                            | After the incident                                 |
| Example | MFA impossible on a legacy app → jump host with MFA and session recording | Ransomware encrypts a server → restore from backup |

<ExamTrap>
  An **audit finding is not an incident**. Finding a weakness and working around it gives you a
  **compensating** control. Repairing damage after a breach gives you a **corrective** one. Many
  candidates answer "corrective" the moment they see the word "after".
</ExamTrap>

A compensating control always has a specific control it substitutes for. If you cannot name that
missing control, it is not one.

## The two axes combine freely

This is what the harder questions check: any type can appear in any category.

| Control                                    | Category    | Type         |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------- | ------------ |
| Full-disk encryption                       | Technical   | Preventive   |
| "Area under surveillance" sign             | Physical    | Deterrent    |
| Daily log review                           | Operational | Detective    |
| Signed acceptable use policy               | Managerial  | Directive    |
| Restore from backup                        | Operational | Corrective   |
| Network segmentation instead of encryption | Technical   | Compensating |

<InPractice>
  One situation almost always contains several controls of different types. A data centre entrance
  with bollards, a vestibule, a badge reader and cameras combines preventive (the vestibule),
  deterrent (visible presence) and detective (the cameras) — all in the physical category. When a
  question describes a whole scene, find **which element** it is asking about.
</InPractice>

## Awareness training is preventive, not detective

A specific case that comes up often. Security awareness training is a **preventive** control — it
aims to stop mistakes before they happen — and it is the canonical example of a preventive control
that is **not** technical.

But timing changes the answer:

- Mandatory training **before** an account is enabled → **preventive**.
- Remedial training assigned **after** somebody fails a phishing test → **corrective**.
- A phishing simulation that **measures** who clicks → **detective**.

## Test yourself

Before moving to the quiz, answer these out loud:

1. A SIEM raises an alert and blocks nothing. Category? Type?
2. A vendor cannot add encryption at rest, so the system is isolated on a dedicated network segment.
   Type?
3. A company requires background checks for new hires. Category of the _requirement document_?
   Category of the _check itself_?

<KeyPoint>
  Answers: (1) technical, detective. (2) compensating — a required control is impossible, so
  something else stands in for it. (3) the requirement is managerial; HR performing it is
  operational.
</KeyPoint>
