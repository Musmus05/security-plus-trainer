## Why this objective matters

Identity and access management questions are rarely acronym quizzes. The exam gives you a new
employee, a transfer, an external partner, a privileged task, or a former worker and asks which
access should exist, who should verify it, and what control enforces the decision. Work through the
identity lifecycle: establish who the person is, give only the rights justified by the job, enforce
those rights when they are used, review them, and remove them when the business reason ends.

It is important to separate proofing, authentication, authorization, and accountability. A person
may prove identity during enrolment, authenticate with a credential at sign-in, and still have no
authorization to open a sensitive record. Similarly, a convenient login experience does not grant
access to every application. Scenario answers should solve the stated IAM failure without adding a
neighbouring control that addresses a different one.

<KeyPoint>
  IAM is a lifecycle: proof the identity, provision the approved access, enforce it at use, attest
  that it remains appropriate, and de-provision it when the need ends.
</KeyPoint>

## Provision and de-provision accounts

<Term>Provisioning</Term> creates an account and its initial entitlements when an employee,
contractor, service, or partner needs access. A good request identifies the identity, the role, the
systems, the approver, and the start date. An account must be connected to a business reason, not
simply created because someone knows an administrator. Automated provisioning can consistently apply
an approved role baseline, but automation does not replace approval or periodic review.

<Term>De-provisioning</Term> removes or disables access when employment, a contract, a project, or a
role ends. Departure is not the only trigger. During a transfer, old permissions should be removed
before or alongside new ones. Keeping old access “just in case” leads to privilege accumulation and
leaves a wider attack surface. In a scenario, urgent removal of a former worker’s access is
de-provisioning; setting up a starter’s account is provisioning.

| Event                    | IAM action                                        | Security result                              |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| New employee starts      | Provision identity and approved baseline rights   | Access is attributable from day one          |
| Employee changes team    | Remove old rights and assign the new role         | Prevents accumulated privilege               |
| Contractor term expires  | Disable accounts and revoke tokens or credentials | Ends access with the business relationship   |
| Temporary project closes | Remove the project entitlement                    | Prevents temporary access becoming permanent |

<ExamTrap>
  Disabling an account is an access decision. Deleting its audit history is different: evidence may
  need to remain available under the organization’s retention policy.
</ExamTrap>

## Assign permissions deliberately

<Term>Authorization</Term> decides what an authenticated identity may do. Reading, modifying,
approving, administering, and accessing a dataset are distinct permissions. Direct assignments can
work for an exception, but groups and roles scale better and make access review understandable.
Apply least privilege: grant no more capability than the task requires. Where one person could both
initiate and approve a sensitive action, separate duties to reduce fraud and error risk.

| Model                                 | How it decides                                                            | Typical cue                                           |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Role-based access control (RBAC)      | Rights follow a job role                                                  | Many people with the same duties need the same access |
| Attribute-based access control (ABAC) | Policy evaluates attributes such as department, device, time, or location | Access changes with contextual facts                  |
| Discretionary access control (DAC)    | Resource owner grants access                                              | A creator shares with named users                     |
| Mandatory access control (MAC)        | Central labels and clearances govern access                               | Classified data follows a fixed hierarchy             |

<Mnemonic>
  RBAC asks “what is your role?” ABAC asks “what facts describe this request?” A role can be an
  attribute, but ABAC can also evaluate device posture, location, and time.
</Mnemonic>

## Establish, connect, and reuse identity

<Term>Identity proofing</Term> happens before an organization accepts a claimed identity as trusted.
It can validate documents, use an in-person process, rely on a trusted sponsor, or follow another
approved method. It is not authentication. Proofing establishes who was enrolled; authentication
checks that the current claimant controls an accepted credential.

<Term>Federation</Term> lets separate organizations or security domains rely on assertions from a
trusted identity provider. An application accepts that provider’s signed assertion rather than
maintaining a separate local password for every external user. <Term>Single sign-on (SSO)</Term> is
the experience of authenticating once and then opening several approved applications. Federation
often enables SSO, but they answer different questions: federation describes a trust relationship;
SSO describes the number of authentication prompts.

<Term>Interoperability</Term> is the capability of IAM components to exchange usable identity and
access information. Compatible standards and formats let a directory, an identity provider, and an
application understand the same identity and attributes. Network connectivity alone is not
interoperability if one system cannot consume the other system’s identity data.

| Term              | Main question                                   | Not the same as                  |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Identity proofing | “Who was enrolled?”                             | A routine login check            |
| Federation        | “Whose identity assertion do we trust?”         | A shared local password database |
| SSO               | “How many times does the user authenticate?”    | Authorization everywhere         |
| Interoperability  | “Can components exchange usable identity data?” | A friendly portal alone          |

<InPractice>
  A supplier signs in to its own organization. Your application accepts an assertion from that
  organization’s identity provider: that is federation. If the supplier can then open several of
  your approved applications without another prompt, that person also experiences SSO.
</InPractice>

## Review access and authenticate strongly

<Term>Attestation</Term> is a formal confirmation, often by a manager or resource owner, that an
existing account or entitlement remains appropriate. It is a review, not the initial request for
access. A manager who periodically certifies a list of people with finance rights is performing
attestation. The review should identify stale accounts, obsolete permissions, and exceptions that no
longer have an owner.

Access controls enforce authorization decisions. They include groups, ACLs, application roles,
policy engines, conditional rules, administrative requirements, and physical measures. Select the
control for the resource and risk. Authentication proves a claimant controls a credential; it does
not by itself decide which files that claimant may read.

<Term>Multifactor authentication (MFA)</Term> uses evidence from at least two different factor
categories: something you know, have, or are. Two passwords are not MFA because both are knowledge
factors. A password plus a hardware key, or a password plus an authenticator-app approval, uses
different categories. MFA reduces the value of a stolen password but does not replace authorization
or the review of inappropriate entitlements.

| Method                              | Factor category    | Key distinction                             |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Password or PIN                     | Something you know | It can be guessed, reused, or phished       |
| Hardware token or authenticator app | Something you have | Possession alone does not identify a person |
| Fingerprint                         | Something you are  | Biometric matching has error considerations |
| Password plus hardware key          | Two categories     | This is MFA                                 |

Password concepts remain relevant where passwords exist. Use long, unique passphrases; prevent reuse
where policy calls for it; store passwords with suitable salted password hashing; and make the reset
process strong enough to re-establish confidence in the claimant. Complexity rules alone do not make
a reused, disclosed, or phished password a strong factor.

## Control privileged work separately

<Term>Privileged access management (PAM)</Term> tools control and monitor elevated accounts, such as
administrator, root, and powerful service accounts. They can vault credentials, require approval,
provide just-in-time elevation, record sessions, and rotate privileged credentials. The purpose is
not to stop administration. It is to ensure powerful access is temporary, authorized, monitored, and
attributable to an individual rather than permanently shared.

<ExamTrap>
  MFA strengthens how an account authenticates. PAM governs how elevated access is requested,
  issued, monitored, and removed. An administrator may need both, and neither replaces the other.
</ExamTrap>

## Test yourself

1. A manager confirms quarterly that every person still needs a finance role. What activity is this?
2. A user signs in once with a trusted identity provider and opens several applications. What
   experience is this?
3. Why should a transfer remove former permissions as well as add new ones?

<KeyPoint>
  1. Attestation. 2. Single sign-on. 3. To prevent privilege accumulation and maintain least
  privilege as the person’s responsibilities change.
</KeyPoint>
