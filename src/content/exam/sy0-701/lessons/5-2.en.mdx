## Why this objective matters

Risk management is how a security programme turns an intimidating list of possible failures into
decisions that leaders can fund, own, and revisit. On the exam, this objective does not ask for a
perfectly safe system. It asks whether you can tell the stages apart: identify a risk, assess and
analyse it, record it, decide how much is acceptable, select a treatment, communicate it, and
understand the business consequence of disruption.

A scenario may deliberately give several true security actions. Choose the one that answers the
question at that point in the process. Finding an exposed database is identification; estimating the
chance and consequence of compromise is assessment; deciding whether to isolate it, insure it, or
live with it is treatment.

<KeyPoint>
  Risk is not the same thing as a vulnerability. A vulnerability is a weakness; risk is the
  possibility that a threat will exploit that weakness and cause an unwanted business outcome.
</KeyPoint>

## Identify, assess, analyse

### Risk identification

Risk identification makes the candidate list. Teams examine assets and business processes, threats,
vulnerabilities, dependencies, prior incidents, suppliers, and control gaps. The output should be
specific enough to act on: “a stolen administrator token could permit changes to payroll records” is
more useful than “authentication is risky.” A risk can arise from a malicious actor, an error, a
failed service, or a natural event; its source does not change the need to manage it.

Identification is not a fix. Discovering that a public storage bucket contains customer files does
not yet say how likely misuse is, how serious it would be, or who has accepted the decision. It only
ensures that the exposure is visible to the process.

### Risk assessment and risk analysis

Assessment gathers the evidence needed to judge a risk. It normally considers likelihood, impact,
the affected asset or process, and existing controls. Analysis uses that information to compare and
prioritise risks. Organisations may use descriptive ratings such as low, medium, and high, or use
numbers and financial estimates. The exam concern is the decision support, not a particular
calculation method.

| Activity            | The question it answers                      | Typical result                                 |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Risk identification | What could go wrong, and why?                | A stated risk with a source and affected asset |
| Risk assessment     | How likely is it and how severe could it be? | Likelihood and impact evidence or ratings      |
| Risk analysis       | Which risks deserve attention first?         | A prioritised view for decision-makers         |

<ExamTrap>
  Do not treat assessment and analysis as synonyms for remediation. A high rating is evidence for a
  decision; applying a control is a later response. Likewise, a vulnerability scan is an input to
  identification and assessment, not a complete risk-management process.
</ExamTrap>

## Make the risk visible

### The risk register

A risk register is the working record of identified risks. It makes ownership and follow-up
possible. Exact fields differ by organisation, but a useful entry connects the description to the
asset or process, likelihood and impact, the chosen treatment, a named owner, status, due dates, and
the remaining or residual risk. It is not merely a ticket queue: it is the shared record that allows
leadership to see what the organisation has decided to carry.

Updating the register matters as much as creating it. A mitigated risk may remain significant, a new
control may reduce likelihood, or a changing business service may increase impact. Closing an entry
because a team is tired of seeing it is neither acceptance nor mitigation; it hides evidence.

### Risk reporting

Risk reporting communicates the register and its trends to the people able to act. A system owner
may need the technical cause and next control, whereas executives need business impact, the amount
of remaining exposure, required funding, and decisions awaiting approval. Good reporting is timely,
clear about uncertainty, and proportionate to the audience. It should highlight changes: a risk that
exceeded its threshold, an overdue treatment, or a dependency that changed the impact.

<InPractice>
  A dashboard showing “twelve high risks” is incomplete if it omits the owners, the affected
  business services, and the requested decisions. Reporting creates accountability; it is not a
  substitute for implementing the selected treatment.
</InPractice>

## Appetite, tolerance, and treatment

These related terms are common distractors because all concern acceptance, but they answer different
questions.

| Term           | Meaning                                                                                       | Exam clue                                 |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Risk appetite  | The broad amount and type of risk the organisation is willing to pursue or retain             | Direction set by leadership               |
| Risk tolerance | The permitted variation or limit around that appetite for a particular objective or situation | A threshold that triggers action          |
| Residual risk  | The exposure that remains after controls or treatment                                         | What still needs acceptance or monitoring |

For example, an organisation might have little appetite for loss of regulated personal data. Its
tolerance could state that an internet-facing service cannot have unresolved critical findings past
a defined deadline. Appetite is the strategic stance; tolerance is the usable boundary. Neither
means that every possible loss is eliminated.

Risk management strategies are the choices made after analysis:

| Strategy | What it means                                                      | Example                                                               |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid    | Stop the activity or change the plan so the risk no longer applies | Do not store a sensitive dataset in the proposed service              |
| Mitigate | Reduce likelihood, impact, or both with controls                   | Add MFA, segmentation, and monitoring to reduce account takeover harm |
| Transfer | Move some financial or contractual consequence to another party    | Use insurance or assign defined liability by contract                 |
| Accept   | Deliberately retain the risk within approved bounds                | Record an approved low-impact risk and monitor it                     |

Transfer does not make the technical threat disappear. An insurer may bear a financial loss, while
the organisation still suffers an outage and must meet its response obligations. Acceptance is an
informed decision by an authorised owner, backed by a recorded residual risk; it is not neglect.

<Mnemonic>
  **AMTA: Avoid, Mitigate, Transfer, Accept.** Ask what happened to the activity, the likelihood or
  impact, the consequence, or the decision to retain it.
</Mnemonic>

## Business impact analysis

A business impact analysis (BIA) starts with business operations rather than a particular threat. It
identifies critical processes, dependencies, and the consequences if they are unavailable or
impaired. Those consequences can include lost revenue, contractual penalties, safety concerns,
regulatory obligations, damaged reputation, or delayed work. The BIA helps establish recovery
priorities and gives risk analysis a business basis for impact ratings.

Do not confuse a BIA with a technical vulnerability assessment. A vulnerability assessment looks for
weaknesses in systems. A BIA asks which business capability matters, how long disruption can be
tolerated, and what depends on it. Both can inform risk decisions, but they begin from opposite ends
of the problem.

<ExamTrap>
  A BIA does not predict the next attack. When a stem asks which payroll, ordering, or emergency
  process must recover first and what its outage costs, it is describing business impact analysis.
</ExamTrap>

## Test yourself

1. A team discovers an exposed administrative interface but has not yet estimated its effect. Which
   risk stage has it completed?
2. Which strategy is being used when a company stops offering a risky feature entirely?
3. What distinguishes risk tolerance from risk appetite?

<KeyPoint>
  (1) Risk identification. (2) Avoidance: the activity is changed or stopped. (3) Appetite is the
  broad strategic willingness to take risk; tolerance is the measurable or situational limit that
  prompts action.
</KeyPoint>
