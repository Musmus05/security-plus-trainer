## Why this objective matters

Security awareness is tested as an operational control, not as a poster in the break room. In a
scenario, the exam expects you to recognise the behaviour an organisation wants, choose guidance
that changes that behaviour, and close the loop through reporting and monitoring. The useful
question is never simply whether employees attended training; it is whether they can spot a risky
moment and get the right people involved quickly.

This objective joins six ideas: phishing, anomalous-behaviour recognition, user guidance and
training, reporting and monitoring, and the development and execution of an awareness practice. The
stems commonly give an employee a vague signal, such as an unexpected request or an unusual login
prompt, then ask for the most useful action.

<KeyPoint>
  Awareness does not make every user a security analyst. It gives people a safe, repeatable path:
  pause, recognise the signal, report it by the approved channel, and let monitoring or the security
  team investigate.
</KeyPoint>

## Phishing: recognise the request, not just the email

Phishing is an attempt to persuade a person to disclose information, approve an action, open
content, or visit a site that helps an attacker. Email is common, but the same persuasion can arrive
by text message, collaboration platform, phone call, or a fake support page. A good awareness
practice therefore teaches the pattern of the request rather than teaching a single sender address
to avoid.

| Signal in the message                   | Why it deserves a pause                                          | Safer response                                            |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unexpected credential or MFA request    | It may be trying to capture a secret or approval                 | Use a known address or application, not the supplied link |
| Urgency or secrecy                      | Pressure discourages verification                                | Slow down and verify through an independent channel       |
| Changed payment or bank details         | A compromised supplier account can make the request look genuine | Confirm using a previously known contact method           |
| Attachment or link out of context       | It may deliver malicious content or lead to impersonation        | Report it; do not open it merely to investigate           |
| A familiar name with an unusual request | Identity can be imitated or an account can be compromised        | Validate the request, not the display name                |

<ExamTrap>
  A message can have correct spelling, a real logo, and a sender the employee has seen before. Those
  details do not prove it is safe. The important distinction is whether the request is expected and
  independently verified.
</ExamTrap>

Phishing simulations can support learning, but a simulation is not an excuse to shame people. Its
value is finding patterns: which group needs clearer guidance, which workflow creates confusion, or
which reporting path is hard to find. The follow-up should explain the cue that was missed and give
the learner a practical response for the next message.

## Recognising anomalous behaviour

Awareness includes events outside a suspicious message. Users are often the first people to notice
that an action does not match normal work. They should recognise anomalies without being asked to
diagnose malware, attribute an attacker, or alter evidence.

| Observation                                              | What makes it anomalous                                         | Appropriate user action                                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Repeated MFA prompts not initiated by the user           | Authentication activity is occurring without the user’s request | Deny the prompts and report them promptly                                         |
| A colleague requests a secret through an unusual channel | The request bypasses the normal process                         | Verify with that colleague independently, then report concern                     |
| Files change names or become unavailable unexpectedly    | Normal access or file behaviour has changed                     | Stop interacting with the suspicious content and contact support or security      |
| An unfamiliar sign-in, device, or location alert         | It differs from the account’s expected pattern                  | Secure the account through the approved process and report the alert              |
| A badge, visitor, or device is where it should not be    | Physical behaviour conflicts with access expectations           | Follow the physical-security reporting procedure rather than confronting unsafely |

<Mnemonic>
  **Unexpected + consequential = verify and report.** An unexpected calendar invitation is one
  thing; an unexpected payment request or MFA approval is consequential. The second must not be
  treated as routine.
</Mnemonic>

## Guidance and training that people can use

Guidance is the concise instruction available at the moment of work. Training builds understanding
and practice over time. They reinforce each other, but they are not interchangeable.

| Practice                | Primary purpose                                                | Example                                                          |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Just-in-time guidance   | Helps a person make one safe decision now                      | A clear report-phishing button and a short instruction beside it |
| Role-based training     | Covers risks specific to a job                                 | Finance staff practise validating payment changes                |
| General awareness       | Establishes common expectations for everyone                   | New employees learn the reporting route and password rules       |
| Exercise or simulation  | Lets learners practise recognition and response                | A harmless phishing exercise followed by feedback                |
| Refresher communication | Reinforces an existing behaviour after change or observed risk | A notice about a new invoice scam targeting suppliers            |

Training should be accessible, relevant, and understandable to its audience. A developer needs
guidance on protecting source code and reporting a suspicious dependency; a receptionist needs a
usable process for visitors and phone requests. Broad policy language alone is weaker than an
example tied to the decision the person must make.

<InPractice>
  If reporting means searching an intranet, writing a long email, and guessing the recipient,
  employees will delay or abandon it. A visible reporting button, a monitored mailbox, or a
  published service-desk route turns the expected response into the easy response.
</InPractice>

## Reporting and monitoring: complete the feedback loop

Reporting is how a possible event reaches people who can investigate. Monitoring is how the
organisation detects patterns, checks that the channel works, and improves the practice. Neither
means asking users to collect evidence by clicking suspicious links or forwarding malicious files to
personal accounts.

An effective report includes the original message or relevant details, the time, the affected
account or device, and what action has already happened. The user should use the organisation’s
approved method, which might preserve useful context automatically. The security team can then
triage the report, look for similar messages or activity, and decide whether broader containment is
necessary.

Monitoring produces useful measures, but a measure must guide a decision. Reporting volume alone
does not prove success: a higher number can mean users now recognise and report more suspicious
activity. Useful interpretation combines reports with simulation outcomes, response time, repeated
themes, and whether people know where to report. Treat measurements as feedback for the programme,
not as a leaderboard for punishment.

## Development and execution

Developing an awareness practice means defining the audience, the behaviour to improve, the content,
delivery methods, reporting route, and measures of success. It also means aligning the material with
current risks and organisational policies. A campaign about an obsolete tool cannot prepare staff
for a newly adopted collaboration workflow.

Executing the practice means delivering it, making participation possible, answering questions,
tracking completion where required, and using feedback to refine the next cycle. A complete record
can show that training was assigned or completed; it does not by itself show that a person can apply
the behaviour. That is why exercises, reporting patterns, and targeted refreshers matter.

<ExamTrap>
  Training completion is an administrative measure. It is not proof that phishing risk disappeared,
  nor does it replace monitoring. The best answer in a scenario often adds a reporting route and
  feedback rather than simply assigning the same annual course again.
</ExamTrap>

## Test yourself

1. An employee receives an unexpected MFA approval prompt. What should the employee do first?
2. Why is an approved reporting channel better than forwarding a suspicious message to a personal
   address?
3. A campaign has a high completion rate but users still mishandle payment-change requests. What
   should the programme change?

<KeyPoint>
  (1) Deny the unrequested prompt and report it through the approved route. (2) The approved route
  preserves context and gets the report to people who monitor and investigate it. (3) Add relevant,
  role-based guidance and practice for independently verifying payment changes, then use the results
  to improve the programme.
</KeyPoint>
