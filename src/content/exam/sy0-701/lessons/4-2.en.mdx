## Why this objective matters

An attacker does not need a sophisticated exploit when an organisation cannot say which laptop,
server, application, account, dataset, or licence it owns. Asset management is a security control
because it gives every later control a target. You cannot patch an unknown host, remove software
that nobody has recorded, investigate an unassigned device, or prove that sensitive data left at the
end of a contract was destroyed.

On the exam, this objective is less about naming an inventory tool than about following an asset
through its life. A scenario may start with a purchase request, an employee receiving a device, a
missing endpoint, or a retired storage array. The question asks what security evidence or action
belongs at that point. The four official topics form one chain: acquisition/procurement,
assignment/accounting, monitoring/asset tracking, and disposal/decommissioning.

<KeyPoint>
  Asset management makes responsibility, location, state, and lifecycle visible. Those facts turn
  policies such as patching, least privilege, data handling, and incident response into actions that
  can actually be carried out.
</KeyPoint>

## Start securely: acquisition and procurement

Security begins before an asset is delivered. The acquisition/procurement process is the point at
which an organisation decides what it will be able to manage later. A request for a new platform,
software subscription, mobile device, or data service should establish the approved purpose and the
party responsible for it. It should also avoid acquiring something that cannot be supported,
updated, inventoried, or retired safely.

Procurement is not simply purchasing. It creates a chance to make security requirements part of the
decision: an approved supplier, a support and update path, ownership information, and a way to
record the asset. A cheap device that never enters the inventory can become an unmanaged entry
point. Software obtained outside the approved process can be unlicensed, unpatched, or invisible to
the team expected to respond to incidents.

| Procurement question                          | Security implication                                                         |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who needs it and for what approved purpose?   | Establishes a business owner and makes unusual use easier to spot.           |
| Can it be supported and updated?              | Avoids deliberately adding an asset that will become exposed when fixes end. |
| Can it be entered into the asset record?      | Ensures later monitoring, audits, and response have an identifier to use.    |
| Does it process or store organisational data? | Connects the purchase to handling and disposal requirements.                 |

<ExamTrap>
  Procurement approval is not the same as access approval. Buying an application permits the
  organisation to obtain it; it does not decide which individual may use its data or administrative
  features. Keep lifecycle governance separate from identity and access decisions.
</ExamTrap>

## Assign it and account for it

Once acquired, an asset has to be assigned and accounted for. Assignment links an asset to a person,
team, service, or owner. Accounting records the facts needed to manage it: what it is, its unique
identifier, where it is, who is responsible, and its current status. The record can cover hardware,
software, and data assets; the exact fields differ, but the security question is the same: can the
organisation identify the asset and the responsible party?

For hardware, the record may connect a serial number or asset tag to a custodian and location. For
software, it may connect a product, version, licence, deployment, and owner. For data, it may name
the system or owner responsible for the collection and identify where it is held. Assignment does
not mean the assignee has unlimited permission. It means there is a known point of accountability
when a device is lost, an application is vulnerable, or a dataset must be handled.

| Term             | What it answers                                       | What it does not prove                                |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Asset assignment | Who is responsible for this asset now?                | That the person has every possible system permission. |
| Asset accounting | What asset exists, with which identifiers and status? | That the asset is currently secure or online.         |
| Inventory        | Which assets are known to the organisation?           | That each record remains accurate without review.     |
| Ownership        | Who makes or accepts lifecycle decisions?             | Who physically happens to use the asset today.        |

<InPractice>
  A laptop handed to a new employee is not fully managed merely because it is in a shipping log. Its
  record should be associated with the recipient or responsible team, and its status should show
  that it has been issued. That makes a later loss report, recovery request, or retirement decision
  traceable.
</InPractice>

## Keep the record alive: monitoring and asset tracking

An inventory is a starting point, not a one-time project. Monitoring and asset tracking compare the
recorded population with the assets actually present and their observed state. They help reveal an
unmanaged endpoint, software installed outside the normal process, an asset in the wrong location,
or a device that should have been returned. They also allow vulnerability management and incident
response to scope their work: which systems run the affected software, who owns them, and where they
are located.

Tracking can be physical or logical. Physical tracking concerns custody and location: which room,
site, employee, or storage area holds the asset. Logical tracking concerns facts such as a device
identity, installed software, service, or data repository. Neither replaces the other. A server can
be visible on a network but still have the wrong owner in the records; a tagged laptop can be known
to finance but no longer report to management tools.

| Observation                                            | Most useful management response                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A device appears on a network but has no asset record. | Investigate the unknown asset, establish ownership, or remove it from service.    |
| A listed system no longer checks in.                   | Confirm its status and location rather than assuming the record is still correct. |
| A critical software flaw is announced.                 | Use software records to identify affected deployments and responsible owners.     |
| A contractor leaves with an issued device.             | Use assignment records to recover or formally transfer the asset.                 |

<Mnemonic>
  Think **what, who, where, state**: what the asset is, who owns it, where it is, and what lifecycle
  state it is in. Tracking is useful when it can answer all four quickly.
</Mnemonic>

## End the lifecycle deliberately: disposal and decommissioning

Disposal/decommissioning is a security event, not an administrative afterthought. An old drive,
retired server, cancelled subscription, or obsolete application can retain organisational data,
credentials, configurations, licences, and network trust. Leaving it connected or merely removing it
from a desk does not end those risks.

Decommissioning is the controlled withdrawal of an asset from service. It should be reflected in the
asset record so that a scanner, audit, or incident team does not mistake a retired asset for a live
one. Disposal concerns the final handling of the physical or digital asset. The correct method
depends on the asset and on applicable organisational requirements, but the security aim remains
consistent: prevent unauthorised recovery or continued use of data and access.

| Lifecycle action                                  | Primary security result                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Remove an asset from active service               | It can no longer provide an unnecessary path into the environment.          |
| Update its status in the asset record             | Teams do not continue to rely on stale ownership or monitoring information. |
| Handle stored organisational data before disposal | Data is not unintentionally transferred with the asset.                     |
| Reconcile licences and subscriptions              | Unneeded software access and cost are not left behind.                      |

<ExamTrap>
  Removing an asset from the inventory is not proof that it was safely disposed of. The record must
  show an appropriate final state, and the asset itself must be handled so its data and access do
  not survive the lifecycle.
</ExamTrap>

## Reading lifecycle scenarios

When a question describes a problem, identify the lifecycle stage first. An unapproved cloud tool
points to acquisition/procurement. A laptop with no named custodian points to assignment/accounting.
A machine discovered during a scan but absent from records points to monitoring/asset tracking. A
retired drive containing company data points to disposal/decommissioning. Then choose the answer
that creates the missing evidence or control at that stage, rather than a neighbouring security
activity that sounds useful.

The objective also rewards seeing the connection between the stages. Good procurement feeds the
inventory. Good assignment makes tracking meaningful. Accurate tracking identifies what must be
retired. Safe decommissioning closes the record. A gap anywhere weakens the ability to protect the
assets everywhere else.
