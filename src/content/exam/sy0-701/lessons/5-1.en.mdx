## Why this objective matters

Security governance makes security decisions repeatable, defensible, and connected to the business.
On the exam, this objective does not ask you to configure a control. It tests whether you can tell
which governing document applies, who should make or carry out a decision, and how an organisation
keeps its rules useful when business, technology, and outside obligations change.

A security control can be technically sound and still fail governance: perhaps nobody owns the data,
the rule was never approved, or a team followed an obsolete procedure. Read scenario questions for
the missing decision, authority, or review cycle—not merely for the technical product named.

<KeyPoint>
  Governance sets direction and accountability. Management implements that direction, and operations
  perform day-to-day work. A policy says what must be true; a procedure says how a person carries
  out a particular task.
</KeyPoint>

## The document hierarchy

The four document types are close neighbours, so the exam can make every option sound reasonable.
Work from the level of detail in the stem. Broad mandatory direction points to a policy; a required,
repeatable technical baseline points to a standard; step-by-step actions point to a procedure.

| Document  | Function                                              | Typical wording | Example                                                    |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Policy    | High-level, management-approved direction             | “must”          | Company data must be classified and protected.             |
| Standard  | Specific mandatory requirement that supports a policy | “must use”      | Administrative access must use multifactor authentication. |
| Procedure | Ordered instructions for performing work              | “first, then”   | Steps for granting a new administrator account.            |
| Guideline | Recommended practice that allows judgment             | “should”        | Developers should use an approved secure coding checklist. |

A policy is not a detailed how-to. It assigns an expectation and authority, such as requiring an
acceptable-use policy or a data-retention policy. Standards make that expectation consistent across
teams. A procedure translates a standard into an action a trained person can perform the same way
tomorrow. Guidelines are useful when circumstances differ: they guide a good decision without
pretending that one precise method fits every situation.

<ExamTrap>
  “Mandatory” alone does not always mean policy. Both policies and standards can be mandatory.
  Choose policy when the statement establishes broad organisational direction; choose standard when
  it fixes a measurable or technical requirement needed to implement that direction.
</ExamTrap>

<Mnemonic>
  Think **P-S-P-G**: Policy sets the purpose, Standard sets the specification, Procedure supplies
  the steps, and Guideline gives guidance.
</Mnemonic>

## External considerations are inputs, not optional reading

Governance has to account for conditions beyond the security team. Laws and regulations may require
particular safeguards or reporting. Contractual commitments can impose security requirements on a
service provider. Industry standards, regulators, court decisions, and customer expectations can
also change what is reasonable for the organisation. A policy that ignores these inputs may be clear
internally yet still leave the business noncompliant or exposed.

Do not merge the terms in a question stem. A law is enforced by a government; a regulation is an
authoritative rule issued under legal authority; a contract binds the parties who sign it; an
industry framework may be voluntary unless a contract or regulator makes it mandatory. The
governance task is to identify the applicable obligation, map it to internal policy and standards,
and retain evidence that the organisation follows them.

| External driver              | Governance response                                                         |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| New privacy requirement      | Update data-handling policy, standards, procedures, and training as needed. |
| Customer security clause     | Assign an owner, implement agreed controls, and monitor the commitment.     |
| Changed threat or technology | Reassess whether current rules still protect the intended assets.           |
| Audit finding                | Correct the gap, document the decision, and verify the correction.          |

<InPractice>
  If a supplier contract requires encryption for stored customer records, a security leader should
  not simply email engineers. The durable response is to connect the obligation to policy, specify
  the required implementation in a standard, update procedures, name owners, and check later that
  the requirement remains in effect.
</InPractice>

## Monitoring and revision keep governance alive

Publishing a policy is a beginning, not proof that it works. Monitoring asks whether people follow
the rules, whether controls meet the intended outcome, and whether external obligations or business
conditions have changed. Sources can include metrics, exception requests, incidents, audit results,
risk assessments, user feedback, and regulatory updates.

Revision follows the evidence. An organisation should define who reviews a document, how often it is
reviewed, who approves a revision, how affected people learn about it, and how the previous version
is retained or retired. Version control and a review date demonstrate that a current document is
intentional rather than forgotten. A revision may tighten a standard after an incident, remove a
requirement made obsolete by a retired service, or clarify a procedure that people consistently
misapply.

Monitoring is not the same as enforcement. Monitoring measures or observes compliance; enforcement
applies a consequence or prevents noncompliant behaviour. Both support governance, but a stem asking
how leaders discover that a policy no longer works is asking about monitoring and review.

<ExamTrap>
  An exception is not a quiet decision to ignore policy. Effective governance records the business
  reason, scope, owner, compensating controls, approver, and expiry or review date. Otherwise a
  temporary exception becomes an undocumented permanent risk.
</ExamTrap>

## Governance structures and decision rights

There is no single correct org chart. What matters is that the structure gives appropriate people
authority, independence, and accountability. A centralised model places major decisions and common
standards with one enterprise function. It increases consistency and visibility, though it can be
less responsive to local needs. A decentralised model gives business units more control. It can
adapt quickly, but duplicate or inconsistent practices are a risk. A hybrid or federated structure
combines central policy and oversight with local implementation.

| Structure        | Strength                                    | Governance risk to watch                        |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Centralised      | Consistent policy and enterprise visibility | Decisions may miss local operating context.     |
| Decentralised    | Local ownership and faster adaptation       | Units may interpret requirements differently.   |
| Hybrid/federated | Central guardrails with local execution     | Decision rights must be explicit to avoid gaps. |

Boards, committees, executives, and designated security leaders commonly establish direction and
accept major risk. System owners, data owners, custodians, privacy staff, legal counsel, and
business units then have distinct roles. The title matters less than the responsibility stated in
the scenario.

## Systems, data, and accountable people

Security governance requires named responsibility. A system owner is accountable for a system’s
business purpose, acceptable risk, and lifecycle decisions. A data owner determines how data is
classified, used, shared, retained, and protected. A custodian handles practical storage, backup,
access administration, or operation of data according to the owner’s requirements. Users must follow
approved rules and report suspected issues; they do not unilaterally redefine data classification.

| Role                | Primary governance responsibility                     | Not the primary responsibility           |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Data owner          | Classify data and decide protection requirements      | Performing every backup or access change |
| Data custodian      | Implement and operate required handling               | Accepting business risk for the data     |
| System owner        | Account for the system’s purpose, risk, and lifecycle | Setting every enterprise-wide policy     |
| Security leadership | Advise, oversee, and define programme direction       | Owning every business dataset            |

<KeyPoint>
  In a role question, locate the decision being made. Classification and permitted use belong with
  the data owner; operational care belongs with the custodian; business accountability for a system
  belongs with its system owner.
</KeyPoint>

## Test yourself

1. A document says every privileged account must use a hardware-backed factor. Is it a policy,
   standard, procedure, or guideline?
2. Which role decides a customer record’s classification: the data owner or the data custodian?
3. What governance activity detects that an approved policy no longer reflects a new external duty?

<KeyPoint>
  1. A standard: it states a specific mandatory requirement. 2. The data owner classifies the data;
  the custodian implements its handling. 3. Monitoring and revision, using inputs such as regulatory
  updates, audits, and risk evidence.
</KeyPoint>
